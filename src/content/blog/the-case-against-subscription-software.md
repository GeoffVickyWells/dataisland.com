Subscriptions make perfect sense for some products. A streaming service consumes ongoing resources. A cloud service incurs ongoing costs. A constantly updated database requires constant maintenance.

Those services genuinely cost money every month. But somewhere along the way, software adopted subscriptions for products that don't necessarily need them. The result has created a curious relationship between developers and customers.

## Ownership Became Access

For decades, buying software meant owning software. You purchased a tool. The tool became yours. It continued working tomorrow. And next year. And often many years after that. Today many products operate differently. Payment stops. Access stops.

The customer never truly owns the software. They rent it. Sometimes indefinitely.

## The Incentive Problem

Subscription software creates different incentives. A traditional purchase encourages developers to build something valuable enough that users willingly recommend it. Subscriptions add another pressure. Retention.

The product must continuously justify an ongoing payment. Sometimes that pressure produces genuine innovation. Sometimes it produces unnecessary features designed to maintain perceived value. More does not always mean better.

## Users Understand Value

One of the software industry's recurring mistakes is assuming customers oppose paying for software. Most people do not. People happily pay for useful products. They object to endless payments for tools they may already consider complete. There is an important difference.

A writer may gladly pay for software that solves a problem. The same writer may question paying again and again for the exact same solution.

## A Simpler Agreement

Software should earn trust. One-time purchases create a remarkably simple agreement. Here is a tool. If you believe it is valuable, buy it. Use it for as long as it remains useful. No account required. No monthly invoice. No surprises.

That model will not fit every category. But it remains a perfectly valid option for many. Perhaps more companies should remember it exists.
