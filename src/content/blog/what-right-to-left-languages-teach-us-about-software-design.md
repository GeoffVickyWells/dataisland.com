Software reveals its assumptions. Most of the time those assumptions remain invisible. Until someone encounters a different way of working. Language exposes those assumptions quickly.

Particularly languages written from right to left.

## The Default User

Much software quietly assumes a default user. English-speaking. Left-to-right. Western keyboard. Western conventions. Often nobody intends to exclude anyone. The assumptions simply go unnoticed. Until the software reaches users who operate differently.

## A Useful Test

Right-to-left languages create an interesting design test. Can the interface adapt? Do controls remain logical? Does text align properly? Does navigation continue to make sense?

Suddenly design decisions that appeared universal reveal themselves as cultural preferences.

## Good Design Adapts

The best software does not insist users adapt to it. It adapts to users. Language direction. Typography. Input methods. Formatting conventions. These should not feel like exceptional situations.

For millions of people they are everyday realities.

## Bigger Lessons

The lesson extends beyond language. Every assumption in software deserves occasional examination. Why does this work this way? Who benefits? Who struggles? Who gets overlooked?

Questions like these produce better products. Not only for minority groups. For everyone.

## Respect Through Design

Supporting different languages is not merely a technical achievement. It is a statement. It says the software recognises that many kinds of people use computers. Many cultures. Many writing systems. Many workflows. And all of them deserve thoughtful tools.

That principle leads to better software. Regardless of language.
