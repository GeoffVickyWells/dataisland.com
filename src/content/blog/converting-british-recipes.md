You've found the perfect recipe for a Victoria sponge or a sticky toffee pudding, and it asks you to bake at "Gas Mark 4, or 160°C fan". If you grew up with Fahrenheit and measuring cups, half of that sentence is a puzzle. Here's what British and Australian recipes mean, and how to convert them.

## Gas Mark

Gas Mark is the temperature scale printed on British gas ovens. Each step is roughly 25°F, and it's easy to convert once you know a few landmarks.

| Gas Mark | °C | °F |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 140 | 275 |
| 2 | 150 | 300 |
| 3 | 170 | 325 |
| 4 | 180 | 350 |
| 5 | 190 | 375 |
| 6 | 200 | 400 |
| 7 | 220 | 425 |
| 8 | 230 | 450 |

The one to remember is **Gas Mark 4 = 180°C = 350°F**: the everyday cake temperature on both sides of the Atlantic.

## Fan ovens

A fan oven (a convection oven in North America) blows hot air around the food, so it cooks faster and more evenly. Recipes compensate by setting the fan temperature about **20°C lower** than a conventional oven. When a British recipe says "180°C, 160°C fan", those are the same bake on two kinds of oven.

The mix-up happens when a recipe gives only one number. Many UK and Australian recipes assume a fan oven by default, so if you bake one at that temperature in a conventional oven, it can come out underdone. Check whether the recipe says "fan", and if your oven has no fan, add about 20°C.

## Spoons and cups aren't the same size everywhere

Spoons look universal, but they aren't:

- A **teaspoon** is 5 mL nearly everywhere.
- A **dessertspoon**, common in British recipes, is 10 mL: two teaspoons.
- A **tablespoon** is 15 mL in the UK and close to that in the US, but **20 mL in Australia**. An Australian recipe calling for a tablespoon of baking powder means a third more than an American tablespoon.
- A **cup** is about 237 mL in the US but 250 mL in Australian and Canadian metric recipes.
- A **pint** is 568 mL in the UK and only 473 mL in the US. A British pint of milk is about 20 percent more than an American one.

## Grams, not cups

Most British recipes measure dry ingredients by weight: 225 g of flour, 175 g of sugar. You can convert these to cups, but you'll lose accuracy, because a cup of flour can weigh anywhere from 120 g to 150 g depending on how you fill it. (We wrote about [why cups and grams don't convert simply](/blog/cups-to-grams-for-baking).) A kitchen scale is the easiest way to follow British baking recipes exactly as written.

## Pan sizes

British tins are usually given in centimetres: a 20 cm round is close to an 8-inch pan, and a 23 cm round is close to a 9-inch one. Close isn't always good enough. A 9-inch round has about 26 percent more area than an 8-inch, so the same batter bakes thinner and faster.
