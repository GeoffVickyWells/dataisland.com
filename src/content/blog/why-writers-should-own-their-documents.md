Writers create strange things. A memo. A proposal. A novel. A research paper. A screenplay. A diary. At first glance they appear very different. But they share one characteristic. They are all intellectual property. They belong to their creators. Or at least they should.

## The Quiet Shift

Many modern workflows encourage writers to think less about files. Everything syncs automatically. Everything happens in the cloud. Everything appears available everywhere. Convenience is appealing. Until the day access changes. An account closes. A subscription lapses. A service disappears.

The writer discovers their relationship with their own work is more complicated than expected.

## Files Still Matter

A document is not an old-fashioned concept. It remains the most direct expression of ownership. Open it. Copy it. Back it up. Move it. Keep it for thirty years.

A file stored under your control remains yours regardless of what happens to a company, platform, or service. That independence has value.

## Longevity Matters

The lifespan of documents often exceeds the lifespan of software. People still open documents created decades ago. Businesses maintain records far longer than product cycles. Authors revisit manuscripts years later.

Researchers reference work from previous careers. Good software respects that reality. It helps create documents. It does not attempt to own them.

## The Writer Comes First

Software should serve writers. Not the reverse. The document is the valuable asset. Everything else is temporary. Applications evolve. Operating systems change. Technology trends come and go. A writer's work remains.

That work deserves to stay under the writer's control.
